#### Abstract

We present a simple, numerically efficient non-parametric method to construct representations of option price surfaces $\hat{C}(T, K)$ which are both smooth and strictly arbitrage-free across time and strike. The method can be viewed as a smooth generalization of the widely-known linear interpolation scheme, and retains the simplicity and transparency of that baseline. Our method is able to provide smooth fits to full option surfaces from 0DTE to the farthest expiry in seconds.

Call option prices for arbitrary strikes $K$ and some expiry $T_{j}$ are represented as convex combinations of Black-Scholes call payoffs anchored at strikes $K_{j}^{i}$ with variances $V_{j}$ with weights $q_{j}$

$$
\hat{C}\left(T_{j}, K\right)=\sum_{i=0}^{N_{j}} q_{j}^{i} \operatorname{Call}\left(K_{j}^{i}, K, V_{j}\right) .
$$

which are free of arbitrage across time and strike. This is extended to the full model $\hat{C}(T, K)$ to include arbitrary non-quoted expiries. The $q_{j}$ 's are shown to have a natural interpretation as discrete space transition densities.

Calibration of the model to observed market quotes on different expiries and strikes than those supporting the model is formulated as a linear program, allowing bid-ask spreads to be incorporated directly via linear penalties or inequalities, and delivering materially lower computational cost than most of the currently available implied-volatility surface fitting routines.

As a further contribution, we derive an equivalent parameterization of the proposed surface in terms of strictly positive "discrete local volatility" [BR15] variables. This yields, to our knowledge, the first construction of smooth, strictly arbitrage-free option price surfaces while requiring only trivial parameter constraints (positivity). We illustrate the approach using S\&P 500 index options.


## 1 Introduction

This article tackles the problem of fitting a discrete set of observed European option bid-ask prices efficiently and develops a smooth, strictly arbitrage-free, non-parametric surface representation of option prices to model prices for a full continuous range of strikes and maturities beyond the observed market quotes. As long as bid/ask spreads of option prices are not zero, and if the market is free of arbitrage, we can always find a version of our model which fits the market. We also

[^0]present a version of our model which requires only positivity of the input parameters, making our model the first known smooth strictly arbitrage-free model whose parameters are subject to only trivial constraints.

Figure 1 makes the strength of the calibration immediately apparent. In a way, these plots speak more than a thousand words: Across the full range of strikes and maturities shown, the model curves closely track the observed S\&P 500 option data, with deviations that are difficult to discern at the scale of the plots and no evident systematic bias in any particular region of the surface. Notably, this high-quality fit is achieved with negligible runtime-solving a single global linear program over the full cross-section of options across all expiries completes essentially instantaneously.

The data used for illustration in this article are end of day option data sourced from Option Metrics IvyDB. ${ }^{1}$. We selected OTM options with at least 100 lots are traded on the day. We also exclude strikes of options whose Vega/ $\sqrt{T}$ is less than $0.1 \%$. Past this, we also only consider expiries with 20 active options.

A convenient feature of our model is that it is strictly arbitrage-free and does not require any of the compromises between fitting and avoiding arbitrage that other currently available approaches rely on: such as Lagrange multipliers, penalties, or reliance on "observations in experiments". Moreover, its implementation is simple and fast enough to be used in high speed trading environments or as part of machine learning models trained using gradient descent or other heavy numerical methods.

Our proposed production variant boils down to writing

$$
\begin{equation*}
\hat{C}\left(T_{j}, K\right):=\sum_{i=0}^{N_{j}} q_{j}^{i} \operatorname{Call}\left(K_{j}^{i}, K, V_{j}\right) . \tag{1}
\end{equation*}
$$

[^1]in terms of martingale densities $q_{1}, \ldots, q_{M}$ for expiries $T_{1}<\cdots<T_{M}$ defined for strikes $K_{j}^{1}< \cdots<K_{j}^{N_{j}}$ and log-normal variances $0<V_{1}<\cdots<V_{M}$. Here, Call $(s, k, v)$ is the Black-Scholes call price function for spot $s$, strike $k$ and variance $v$. We find the densities $q_{1}, \ldots, q_{M}$ to match the market bid/ask using linear programming.

The method enforces absence of arbitrage in both time and strike space for the marginals $\hat{C}\left(T_{k}, \cdot\right)$. In order to retain the linear structure of our approach with respect to $q$ we postulate that the model price for an arbitrary expiry $T \in\left(T_{j-1}, T_{j}\right]$ and strike $K$ can be written in terms of some increasing weight function $\alpha_{j}(T) \in[0,1]$ with $\alpha_{j}\left(T_{j-1}\right)=0$ and $\alpha_{j}\left(T_{j}\right)=1$ as

$$
\hat{C}(T, K):=\left\{\begin{align*}
\alpha_{j}(T) & \sum_{i=1}^{N_{j}} q_{j}^{i} \operatorname{Call}\left(K_{j}^{i}, K, V(T)\right)  \tag{2}\\
+\left(1-\alpha_{j}(T)\right) & \sum_{i=1}^{N_{j-1}} q_{j-1}^{i} \operatorname{Call}\left(K_{j-1}^{i}, K, V(T)\right)
\end{align*}\right.
$$

where we use the interpolated variance

$$
V(T):=\alpha_{j}(T) V_{j}+\left(1-\alpha_{j}(T)\right) V_{j-1}
$$

Note that this preserves smoothness in $K$ for all $T>0$. An example for $\alpha_{j}(T) \in[0,1]$ is the linear

$$
\alpha_{j}(T):=\frac{\left(T \wedge T_{j}-T_{j-1}\right)^{+}}{T_{j}-T_{j-1}}
$$

In practice a $C^{1}$ monotone spline is preferable. (In the above $N_{0}=1, q_{0}^{1}:=1$ and $K_{1}^{1}:=1$.)

## Fitting to Market Data

Assume now our market has expiries $0=\tau_{0}<\cdots<\tau_{m}$ with market strikes $k_{\ell}^{r}$ for $\ell=1, \ldots, m$ and $r=1, \ldots, n_{\ell}$. The model price $\hat{C}\left(\tau_{\ell}, k_{\ell}^{r}\right)$ for each $k_{\ell}^{r}$ with $\tau_{\ell} \in\left(T_{j-1}, T_{j}\right]$ given in (2) is linear in the variables $q$, hence we can fit $q$ to market bid/ask prices by linear/convex programming provided our objective is linear/convex. This is discussed in section 4.2 on implementation on page 20.

Our model fits market data surprisingly well and is by construction strictly free of arbitrage and smooth. Our model has a simple interpretation as a discrete jump process multiplied by an underlying martingale process.

In addition to our production model, we also present extensions to other underlying processes than Black-Scholes, and generalize our approach to a theoretically more appealing model which allows specification of different variance curves $V_{j}^{i}$ per strike. While this model is theoretically more attractive its calibration is much heavier and it is therefore perhaps of more academic interest.

## Accessible Parametrization

Considering the increased interest in generative models for option prices we also present a parametrization of our model in terms of a parameter set which is subject only to positivity constraints. To this end we express the densities $q_{1}, \ldots, q_{M}$ in our formula (which are subject to linear but still complicated constraints) with densities generated by "Discrete Local Volatilities" (DLVs) as introduced in [BR15] (which only need to be non-negative). These DLVs together with the forward volatilities to generate the backbone variances only need be positive to generate an arbitrage-free smooth call option price surface.

We note that a model in that space, combined with [BMPW22a, BMPW22b] will yield not only arbitrage-free snapshots, but also discrete time dynamics which are free of dynamic arbitrage. Such a model is subject to further research.

## Related Work

There is an extensive body of work focusing on interpolating option implied volatilities with the aim of ensuring absence if strike and calendar arbitrage, starting as early as [May95] and more recently [BP04, Fen09, Des26]. However, none of the proposed models is able to ensure strict absence of arbitrage.

Our approach is a generalization of the linear fit to market prices first discussed in [BR15]. This was more recently expanded in [CRW20a, CRW20b] to fit a linear arbitrage-free model between bid/ask prices. Both works expand on the similar, but much heavier ideas of [Bue06] where a linear model is fitted to the market which also reprices forward start options. The underlying linear model had - in effect - been already been discussed in the seminal [ABR97, AH21], albeit not in the context of fitting to the market, but in the context of developing a consistent pricing operator once an arbitrage-free set of option prices is obtained.

In fact, linear interpolation already addresses many of the challenges of fitting a model to an arbitrary surface of observed option prices. When applied to a dense grid it can be used to regularize local volatilities as discussed in [BR15]. However, linear interpolation suffers from being "the most expensive" interpolation for call and put prices as was pointed out in [Bue06]. This means that implied volatilities of linearly interpolated option prices is too large as is illustrated in figure 2. This makes linear interpolation particularly unsuitable if the option surface is used in automated trading applications such as Deep Hedging [BGTW19] or other methods which use the prices of the surface to make trading decisions. A smooth interpolation scheme avoids "too expensive" prices.

Our underlying model formulation is similar to [EA23] who uses a time-homogeneous grid and assumes absence of arbitrage in the market prices to construct the market density upon which he then attempts to train per-strike variances via a non-linear least-squares optimisation. Using different variances per strike means that the resulting model no longer avoids term-structure arbitrage, a topic which we comment on below.

Seemingly similar are Gaussian mixture models which have been covered in the literature in the past, chiefly [BMR02] and their earlier [BM00]. Their primary focus is the construction of a local volatility diffusion which mimics the marginal distributions generated by Gaussian mixture models. The central difference, apart from the difference in focus, of these works to ours is that in [BM00, BMR02] the mixing is performed with fixed weights over time over a number of processes which all start at the same point; while our solution works by jumps from one underlying stock price to another: The latter difference can be stated more explicitly, in our work as

$$
\hat{C}\left(T_{j}, K\right):=\sum_{i=0}^{N_{j}} q_{j}^{i} \operatorname{Call}\left(K_{j}^{i}, K, V_{j}\right)
$$

where we used $S_{0}=1$ for spot and variances $V_{j}$. Mixture models on the other hand implement

$$
\sum_{i=0}^{N_{j}} q_{j}^{i} \operatorname{Call}\left(1, K, V_{j}\right)
$$

The effect is that we can impose much stronger skew via the weighting functions $q_{j}$, at the cost of needing to limit the weights $q_{j}$ to densities with unit mean. As a result, we are able to fit any grid of arbitrage-free bid/ask prices, while Gaussian mixture models typically fail to do so.

We focus here on non-parametric models which aim to fit a market, rather than on (low-) parametric representations of the option surface which express a view on the market through the lens of intuitive and statistically meaningful parameters. Usually such models exhibit arbitrage - with the exception of the SSVI models presented in the seminal [GJ14]. This is a great tool to express a view on the market through a low-parametric model lens. However, as the the authors point out, the most accessible parametrization does not always fit well to observed option markets which means they need to resort to more involved numerical methods to fit their low-parametric model to observed market data while retaining strict absence of arbitrage.

## Classic Calibration Models

We omitted in this brief summary all methods which rely on calibrating a heavier dynamic model such as Heston [Hes93], Bates [Bat93], or various Levy models e.g. [CN17, CGMY02]. In each of these cases model parameters are trained by minimizing the distance between market and model prices using numerical methods. The models used for this purpose in practise tend to be affine, in which case the Fast-Fourier pricing methods pioneered in [CM99] can be applied during calibration.

Further advances were made in [CN12] where the authors show how to fit Levy models to an already arbitrage-free market. Such methods are very promising but do not yet achieve the same quality of fit with the same speed as SANOS.

## 2 Arbitrage-Free Call Prices

We start by fixing some notions and conventions that we will frequently need throughout the paper. For this we first briefly recall the Black-Scholes formula in the form we will frequently use in the paper: With $s>0$ denoting spot, $k \geq 0$ strike and $v \geq 0$ (total) variance ${ }^{2}$, the formula for European call options is given in terms of the standard normal distribution function $\mathcal{N}$ as

$$
\begin{equation*}
\operatorname{Call}(s, k, v):=s \mathcal{N}\left(d_{+}\right)-k \mathcal{N}\left(d_{-}\right) \quad \text { with } \quad d_{ \pm}=\frac{-\ln (k / s) \pm \frac{1}{2} v}{\sqrt{v}} . \tag{3}
\end{equation*}
$$

In this article we discuss construction of smooth arbitrage-free European option price surfaces for an equity denoted by $S$. The following assumptions hold throughout the paper unless otherwise stated. We will assume that the dynamics of the equity $S$ can be written in the form $S_{T}=F_{T} Z_{T}$ where $F$ is the forward curve ${ }^{3}$ and where $Z$ is a martingale with unit expectation. We also assume that the equity does not default and does not otherwise reach zero ${ }^{4}$. The deterministic discount factors are given by $\mathrm{DF}_{T}$. The key point is that in this setting if $\mathcal{C}(T, \mathcal{K}) \equiv \mathrm{DF}_{T} \mathbb{E}\left[\left(S_{T}-\mathcal{K}\right)_{+}\right]$is a surface of call prices with expiries $T$ and cash strikes $\mathcal{K}$, then we can convert these into "pure" call prices on the martingale $Z$ by setting $\mathbb{E}\left[\left(Z_{T}-K\right)_{+}\right] \equiv C(T, K):=\mathcal{C}\left(T, F_{T} K\right) /\left(\mathrm{DF}_{T} F_{T}\right)$.

A similar transformation is applied to obtain pure put prices, which we then convert into pure call prices by put-call-parity. With these preparations, we can henceforth focus or attention on

[^2]"pure" call prices, i.e. those written on the underlying martingale process:

Definition 2.1 (Arbitrage Free Call Price Surfaces). We call a function $C:[0, \infty)^{2} \rightarrow[0, \infty)$ an arbitrage-free call price function (or "surface") if there is a probability measure $\mathbb{P}$ and a positive $\mathbb{P}$-martingale $\left(Z_{T}\right)_{T \in[0, \infty)}$ with $Z_{0}=1$ a.s., such that ${ }^{5}$

$$
C(T, K)=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[\left(Z_{T}-K\right)^{+}\right]
$$

In this case we call $Z$ the martingale representation of $C$.
We will by convention consider derivatives of call prices in $K$ as right hand derivatives ${ }^{6}$. Next, we state a theorem that formulates a list of shape requirements on a surface, which guarantee to yield an arbitrage free call price surface in the sense of definition 2.1.

Theorem 2.2. A call price surface $C:[0, \infty)^{2} \rightarrow[0, \infty)$ arbitrage-free if and only if

1. The market has unit expectation: $C(T, 0) \equiv \mathbb{E}\left[Z_{T}\right]=1$ for all $T$;
2. zero is unattainable (i.e. no atom at 0 ): $\partial_{K} C(T, 0) \equiv-1$;
3. call prices ultimately reach zero: $\lim _{K \uparrow \infty} C(T, K)=0$,
4. call prices are convex: $\partial_{K K} C(T, K) \geq 0$; and
5. call prices are increasing in time: $\partial_{T} C(T, K) \geq 0$.

As a consequence we also have:
6. $C(T, K) \geq(1-K)_{+}$for any $K, T$; as a direct consequence of 1. \& 5. and
7. $\partial_{K} C(T, K) \in[-1,0)$ for any $K, T$.

Remark 2.3. Several of the above conditions are well-known in the context of related literature. In several related settings, strict absence of arbitrage statements are not always fully complete: the boundary conditions 1. and 3. are frequently omitted, and/or negativity of the derivative 7. is not required.

Our set of conditions for theorem 2.2 are minimal equivalent conditions for the existence of a positive true martingale in sense of definition 2.1. A full proof is delegated to the appendix page 26ff.
Remark 2.4. To ensure positivity, we have imposed condition 2. rather than the commonly used weaker condition $\partial_{K} C(T, 0) \geq-1 .^{7}$ This requirement is justified as we consider an asset reaching zero outside a default event undesirable, and point to [Bue08] on how to incorporate "sudden" default risk in our framework.

Definition 2.5 (Smooth call price function). We call a call price function smooth (in strike direction) if $\partial_{K K} C(T, K)>0$ for all $K>0$.

### 2.1 Discrete Martingales

In this section we recall results from [BR15] which we also use to fix notation and ideas for the remainder of the paper. The purpose of this section is to anchor our model in its non-smooth limit of linear interpolation between model prices.

Assume therefore for now we are given expiries $0=T_{0}<T_{1}<\cdots<T_{M}$ each with $N_{j}$ strikes $0=K_{j}^{0}<K_{j}^{1}<\cdots<1<\cdots<K_{j}^{N_{j}}$ and call prices $C_{j}^{0}, \ldots, C_{j}^{N_{j}}$ where $C_{j}^{i}$ denotes the price of

[^3]the call expiring in $T_{j}$ with strike $K_{j}^{i}$. We also assume w.l.g. (see remark 2.7) that the boundary strikes $K_{j}^{1} \equiv K^{\min }$ and $K_{j}^{N_{j}} \equiv K^{\max }$ are all the same. Define for $i=0, \ldots, N_{j}-1$

$$
\begin{equation*}
d C_{j}^{i}:=\frac{C_{j}^{i+1}-C_{j}^{i}}{K_{j}^{i+1}-K_{j}^{i}} \tag{4}
\end{equation*}
$$

and set $d C_{j}^{N_{j}}:=0$. We wish to understand when this set of market data is "arbitrage-free":
Theorem 2.6. A market of call prices $C_{j}^{i}$ is arbitrage-free in the sense that there exists a positive (discrete state) martingale $X$ with unit expectation which satisfies $C_{j}^{i}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[\left(X_{T_{j}}-K_{j}^{i}\right)^{+}\right]$if

1. the market has unit expectation: $C_{j}^{0}=1$;
2. zero is unattainable: $d C_{j}^{0}=-1$ which with the above and (4) implies $C_{j}^{1}=1-K^{\min }$, 8
3. call prices ultimately reach zero: $C_{j}^{N_{j}}=0$;
4. call prices are convex: $d C_{j}^{i} \leq d C_{j}^{i+1}$; and
5. call prices are increasing in time when linearly interpolated in the following sense: for $K_{j+1}^{i} \in\left[K_{j}^{\ell}, K_{j}^{\ell+1}\right)$ we have

$$
\begin{equation*}
C_{j}^{\ell} \frac{K_{j}^{\ell+1}-K_{j+1}^{i}}{K_{j}^{\ell+1}-K_{j}^{\ell}}+C_{j}^{\ell+1} \frac{K_{j+1}^{i}-K_{j}^{\ell}}{K_{j}^{\ell+1}-K_{j}^{\ell}} \leq C_{j+1}^{i} \tag{5}
\end{equation*}
$$

If the strikes are homogeneous across expiries this reduces to the simpler

$$
d C_{j}^{i} \leq d C_{j+1}^{i}
$$

As a consequence we also have
6. $C_{j}^{i} \geq\left(1-K_{j}^{i}\right)^{+}$and
7. $d C_{j}^{i} \in[-1,0]$.

Proof. See [BR15] theorems 3.1 and 3.2 and subsequent statements there for a constructive proof of the existence of a martingale $X$ with these marginal distributions in the general case of nonhomogeneous grids. It is also shown there that the corresponding martingale $X$ only has mass in $K_{j}^{1}, \ldots, K_{j}^{N_{j}}$.

Remark 2.7. A common practice ${ }^{9}$ when working with real market data to satisfy the conditions on the strikes in the previous theorem is to first consider strikes $0 \ll K_{j}^{2}<\cdots<1<\cdots<K_{j}^{N_{j}-1}$ with calls $C_{j}^{i}>\left(1-K_{j}^{i}\right)^{+}$and $1>C_{j}^{2}>\cdots>C_{j}^{N_{j}-1}>0$ which satisfy $-1<d C_{j}^{2} \leq \cdots \leq d C_{j}^{N_{j}-1}<0$, and then to insert the missing data as follows:

1. $K_{j}^{0}:=0$ with $C_{j}^{0}:=1$;
2. $K_{j}^{1}:=K^{\min } \ll \min _{j} K_{j}^{2}$ with $C_{j}^{1}:=1-K^{\min }$ such that $-1=d C_{j}^{0}<d C_{j}^{1}<d C_{j}^{2}$ for all $j$;
3. $K_{j}^{N}:-K^{\max } \gg \max _{j} K_{j}^{N_{j}-1}$ with $C_{j}^{N_{j}}=0$ such that $d C_{j}^{N_{j}-2}<d C_{j}^{N_{j}-1}<0$ for all $j$.
[^4]Given a discrete state martingale $X$ as per above, let us denote with slight abuse of notation by $X_{j}:=X_{T_{j}}$ the corresponding discrete state and discrete time martingale Markov chain. Given strikes $K_{j}=\left(K_{j}^{1}, \ldots, K_{j}^{N_{j}}\right)^{\prime}$ and a Markov chain $\left(X_{j}\right)_{j=0, \ldots, M}$ with $\mathbb{P}\left(X_{j}=K_{j}^{i}\right)=: p_{j}^{i}$, define the transition probabilities

$$
\begin{equation*}
P_{j \mid j-1}^{i \mid \ell}:=\mathbb{P}\left[X_{j}=K_{j}^{i} \mid X_{j-1}=K_{j-1}^{\ell}\right], \quad i=1, \ldots, N_{j} ; \ell=1, \ldots, N_{j-1} . \tag{6}
\end{equation*}
$$

Let $P_{j \mid j-1} \in \mathbb{R}^{N_{j} \times N_{j-1}}$ denote the matrix with entries $\left(P_{j \mid j-1}\right)_{i, \ell}=P_{j \mid j-1}^{i \mid \ell}$. (Thus columns correspond to the conditioning state $\ell$.)
Definition 2.8 (Martingale transition operator). A matrix $P \in \mathbb{R}^{N_{j} \times N_{j-1}}$ is called a (discrete) martingale transition operator with respect to strikes $K_{j}$ and $K_{j-1}$ if

1 . it is column-stochastic: $P \geq 0$ and $1^{\prime} \cdot P=1^{\prime}$;
2. it satisfies the martingale property: $K_{j}^{\prime} \cdot P=K_{j-1}^{\prime}$,
where we used "." to denote matrix/vector multiplications. Equivalently, for each conditioning index $\ell \in\{1, \ldots, N\}$, the above properties translate into

$$
\sum_{i=1}^{N_{j}} P^{i \mid \ell}=1, \quad \text { and } \quad \sum_{i=1}^{N_{j}} K_{j}^{i} P^{i \mid \ell}=K_{j-1}^{\ell} .
$$

Definition 2.9 (Martingale Density). A sequence $p=\left(p_{j}\right)_{j=0, \ldots, M}$ of densities $p_{j} \in \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{N}$ each defined for expiries $0=T_{0}<\cdots<T_{M}$ over strikes $K_{j}$ with unit mean is called a martingale density if there exist ${ }^{10}$ martingale transition operators $P_{j \mid j-1} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times N}$ such that

$$
p_{j}=P_{j \mid j-1} p_{j-1}
$$

written out as

$$
p_{j}^{i_{j}}=\sum_{i_{j-1}=1}^{N_{j-1}} P_{j \mid j-1}^{i_{j} \mid i_{j-1}} p_{j-1}^{i_{j-1}}
$$

(The initial density $p_{0}=(1)$ is trivially defined for $N_{0}=1$ and strike $K_{0}^{1}:=1$ ).

\end{equation*}
$$

### 2.2 Linear Arbitrage-Free Call Price Surfaces

We now recall the well-known result that linear interpolation in strike and expiry yields an arbitrage-free - but not smooth - interpolator, to motivate our later results.

Definition 2.11 (Linear Interpolation). The linear interpolator in strikes $K$ for expiry $T_{j}$ is

$$
\bar{C}_{j}(K):=\sum_{i=0}^{N_{j}-1}\{d C_{j}^{i}(\underbrace{K-K_{j}^{i+1}}_{\leq 0})+C_{j}^{i+1}\}_{K_{j}^{i} \leq K<K_{j}^{i+1}} .
$$

with the trivial $\bar{C}_{0}(K):=(1-K)^{+}$. To interpolate in time without causing calendar arbitrage, any increasing interpolation along fixed strikes will be sufficient. Assume therefore that $\alpha_{j}$ : $\left(T_{j-1}, T_{j}\right] \rightarrow[0,1]$ is increasing with with $\alpha_{j}\left(T_{j-1}\right)=0$ and $\alpha_{j}\left(T_{j}\right)=1$. Then let

$$
\begin{equation*}
\bar{C}(T, K):=\bar{C}_{j}(K) \alpha_{j}(T)+\left(1-\alpha_{j}(T)\right) \bar{C}_{j-1}(K) \tag{8}
\end{equation*}
$$

[^5]Remark 2.12. A naive choice for the time-interpolation functions $\alpha_{j}$ is linear interpolation

$$
\begin{equation*}
\alpha_{j}(T):=\frac{T \wedge T_{j}-T_{j-1}}{T_{j}-T_{j-1}} . \tag{9}
\end{equation*}
$$

A common alternative is to interpolate smoothly in implied Black-Scholes ATM variance. To this end let $W_{j}$ be the Black-Scholes implied variance for $C_{j}(1)$. Define an increasing interpolation function $W(T)$ with $W\left(T_{j}\right)=W_{j}$ for example an interpolating monotone $C^{1}$ spline ${ }^{11}$ Then set

$$
\alpha_{j}(T):=\frac{\operatorname{Call}(1,1, W(T))-C_{j-1}(1)}{C_{j}(1)-C_{j-1}(1)} \mathbb{1}_{T \in\left(T_{j-1}, T_{j}\right]} .
$$

For simplicity we will continue refer to $\bar{C}$ as "linear interpolation" even if $\alpha$ may not be linear.
Proposition 2.13. It holds that

$$
\begin{equation*}
\bar{C}_{j}(K)=\sum_{i=1}^{N} p_{j}^{i}\left(K_{j}^{i}-K\right)^{+} . \tag{10}
\end{equation*}
$$

with the martingale density $p$ as defined in (7).
Proof. We notice that

$$
C_{j}^{i+1}=\left(C_{j}^{i+1}-C_{j}^{i+2}\right)+\left(C_{j}^{i+2}-C_{j}^{i+3}\right)+\cdots+\left(d C_{j}^{N-1}-d C_{j}^{N}\right)=\sum_{\ell=i+1}^{N-1} d C_{j}^{\ell}(\underbrace{K_{j}^{\ell}-K_{j}^{\ell+1}}_{\leq 0})
$$

hence

$$
\begin{align*}
\bar{C}_{j}(K) & =\sum_{i=0}^{N_{j}-1} \mathbb{1}_{K_{j}^{i} \leq K<K_{j}^{i+1}}\{d C_{j}^{i}(\underbrace{K-K_{j}^{i+1}}_{\leq 0})+\sum_{\ell=i+1}^{N_{j}-1} d C_{j}^{\ell}(\underbrace{K_{j}^{\ell}-K_{j}^{\ell+1}}_{\leq 0})\} \\
& =\sum_{i=0}^{N_{j}-1} d C_{j}^{i}\left(\left(K_{j}^{i}-K\right)^{+}-\left(K_{j}^{i+1}-K\right)^{+}\right)  \tag{11}\\
& =\underbrace{d C_{j}^{0}\left(K_{j}^{0}-K\right)^{+}}_{=0}-d C_{j}^{N_{j}-1}\left(K_{j}^{N_{j}}-K\right)^{+}+\sum_{i=1}^{N_{j}-1}(\underbrace{d C_{j}^{i}-d C_{j}^{i-1}}_{\geq 0})\left(K_{j}^{i}-K\right)^{+} \\
& =\sum_{i=1}^{N_{j}}(\underbrace{d C_{j}^{i}-d C_{j}^{i-1}}_{=p_{j}^{i} \geq 0})\left(K_{j}^{i}-K\right)^{+}=(10) \tag{12}
\end{align*}
$$

where we used that we had defined $d C_{j}^{N_{j}}:=0$.
The fact that $p_{j}$ is non-negative and sums up to 1 is clear. To show that $p_{j}$ has unit expectation follows from $\bar{C}_{j}(0)=1$.

Proposition 2.14. Assume the market is arbitrage-free in the sense of theorem 2.6.
Then, linear interpolation $\bar{C}$ is arbitrage-free.
Proof.

1. Equation (11) shows $\bar{C}_{j}(0)=\sum_{i=0}^{N-1} d C_{j}^{i}\left(K_{j}^{i}-K_{j}^{i+1}\right)=0-(-1)=1$.
2. With $\left.\partial K\left(K_{j}^{i}-K\right)^{+}\right|_{K=0}=\mathbb{1}_{K>K_{j}^{0}}$ we also see $\left.\partial_{K} \bar{C}_{j}(K)\right|_{K=0}=d C_{j}^{0}=-1$.
3. $\lim _{K \uparrow \infty} \bar{C}_{j}(K)=0$ is satisfied by constriction since $C_{j}\left(K_{j}^{N_{j}}\right)=0$.

[^6]4. Equation (10) shows that $\bar{C}_{j}$ is convex.
5. Considering (5) and (8) $\bar{C}$ is increasing in $T$. Note that a convex combination of two convex functions remains convex.

## Why not Stay Linear

Linear interpolation is very natural, and has the desirable property to exist if and only if the market (on a suitable strike grid) is arbitrage-free. However, as pointed out in [Bue06], linear interpolation is "the most expensive" interpolation of option prices which is consistent with the anchor prices.

In practice that means implied volatilities between interpolated strikes is too high. Figure 2 illustrates this effect with an example on real data.

## 3 Smooth Arbitrage-Free Call Price Surfaces

In this section we present the main results of this article: While linear interpolation is a simple and powerful tool construct arbitrage-free call price surfaces, it essentially represents a state-discrete underlying process. Indeed, $\bar{C}$ is not smooth with $\partial_{K K} \bar{C}(T, K)=0$ for all $K \notin\left\{K_{1}^{1}, \ldots, K_{M}^{N_{M}}\right\}$ or $T \notin\left\{T_{1}, \ldots, T_{M}\right\}$. The density $p(T, K)$ for the full surface can only have mass in ( $T, K_{j}^{i}$ ).

In most practical applications we aim to have a smooth representation of call price surfaces which allows pricing any option at any strike and expiry. Linear interpolation might be mathematically valid, but it is also extreme: indeed, linear interpolation of a set of arbitrage-free market prices provides "the most expensive interpolation scheme available" [Bue06]. That means it is not helpful for inferring insights on values of options between quoted strikes and expiries. From a practical perspective a relatively sparse linear interpolator does not lend itself well to the use of continuous state methods, chiefly Dupire's famous local volatility [ $\mathrm{D}^{+} 94$ ], given on a smooth grid as

$$
\sigma(T, K)^{2}:=2 \frac{\partial_{T} C(T, K)}{K^{2} \partial_{K K} C(T, K) d T} .
$$

(In [BR15] we presented with "discrete local volatilities" an alternative to Dupire's local volatility which works with large time steps and discrete market data.)

We now present an alternative to interpolate a set of arbitrage-free market call prices with smooth functions such that the resulting surface is itself also smooth. We start with the following reference model which performs surprisingly well despite its simplicity. We managed to fit it to a wide range of SPX market data. Section 4 on page 19 discusses implementation.

Theorem 3.1 (Smooth Call Prices). Let $Y=\left(Y_{t}\right)_{t \geq 0}$ be an atomless positive martingale with full support over $(0, \infty)$ and unit mean. Set $Y_{j}:=Y_{T_{j}}$. Let $q=\left(q_{0}, \ldots, q_{M}\right)$ be an at martingale density as defined in definition 2.9 and let

$$
\begin{equation*}
\hat{C}_{j}(K):=\sum_{i=1}^{N} q_{j}^{i} \mathbb{E}\left[\left(K_{j}^{i} Y_{j}-K\right)^{+}\right] \tag{13}
\end{equation*}
$$

Using a time-interpolation scheme $\alpha$ such as (8) we define call prices for expiries $T \in\left(T_{j-1}, T_{j}\right]$ as

$$
\begin{equation*}
\hat{C}(T, K):=\alpha_{j}(T) \sum_{i=1}^{N_{j}} q_{j}^{i} \mathbb{E}\left[\left(K_{j}^{i} Y_{T}-K\right)^{+}\right]+\left(1-\alpha_{j}(T)\right) \sum_{i=1}^{N_{j-1}} q_{j-1}^{i} \mathbb{E}\left[\left(K_{j-1}^{i} Y_{T}-K\right)^{+}\right] \tag{14}
\end{equation*}
$$

For $T>T_{M}$ we extrapolate using $Y$ :

$$
\begin{equation*}
\hat{C}(T, K):=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{M}} q_{j}^{i} \mathbb{E}\left[\left(K_{M}^{i} Y_{T}-K\right)^{+}\right] \tag{15}
\end{equation*}
$$

Then $\hat{C}$ is a smooth call price function.
Moreover, if $q=p$, then $\hat{C}_{j} \geq \bar{C}_{j}$ with equality if $Y \equiv 1$ in which case $\hat{C}$ reduces to linear interpolation.
The discrete-time martingale representation for $\hat{C}_{0}, \ldots, \hat{C}_{M}$ is given by

$$
\begin{equation*}
Z_{j}:=X_{j} Y_{j} \tag{16}
\end{equation*}
$$

in the sense that $\mathbb{E}\left[\left(Z_{j}-K\right)^{+}\right]=\hat{C}_{j}(K)$ where $X \sim q$ is the discrete state Markov martingale (6).
Proof of the theorem. Strictly speaking (16) is sufficient to prove that $\hat{C}$ is arbitrage-free. For clarity with still provide a brief proof:

1. $\hat{C}_{j}(0)=\sum_{i} q_{j}^{i} K_{j}^{i} \mathbb{E}\left[Y_{j}\right]=1$ because $q_{j}$ and $Y_{j}$ has unit mean.
2. $\partial_{K} \hat{C}_{j}(0)=-\sum_{i} q_{j}^{i} \mathbb{P}\left[K_{j}^{i} Y_{j}>0\right]=-1$ because $Y_{j}>0$ and $q_{j}$ has unit mean.
3. $\lim _{K \uparrow \infty} \hat{C}_{j}(K)=0$ because $\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{j}\right]=1$ and dominated convergence applies.
4. $\partial_{K K} \hat{C}_{j}(K)>0$ by construction for $K>0$.
5. To show that call prices are increasing in time, consider

$$
\begin{aligned}
\sum_{i=1}^{N_{j+1}} q_{j+1}^{i} \mathbb{E}\left[\left(K_{j+1}^{i} Y_{j+1}-K\right)^{+}\right] & \stackrel{(*)}{\geq} \sum_{i=1}^{N_{j+1}} q_{j+1}^{i} \mathbb{E}\left[\left(K_{j+1}^{i} Y_{j}-K\right)^{+}\right] \\
& \stackrel{d \mathbb{P}^{Y}}{=}=Y d \mathbb{P} \\
& \stackrel{(* *)}{\geq} \mathbb{E}^{Y}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{N_{j+1}} q_{j+1}^{i}\left(K_{j+1}^{i}-K / Y_{j}\right)^{+}\right] \\
& =\mathbb{E}^{Y}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{N_{j}} q_{j}^{i}\left(K_{j}^{i}-K / Y_{j}\right)^{+}\right] \\
& \sum_{i=1}^{N_{j}} \mathbb{E}\left[\left(K_{j}^{i} Y_{j}-K\right)^{+}\right]
\end{aligned}
$$

where ( $*$ ) follows because $Y$ is a martingale, and ( $* *$ ) follows as before because $q$ is a martingale density which means, in particular,

$$
\sum_{i=1}^{N_{j+1}} q_{j+1}^{i}\left(K_{j+1}^{i}-K\right)^{+} \geq \sum_{i=1}^{N_{j}} q_{j}^{i}\left(K_{j}^{i}-K\right)^{+}
$$

for all $K$. Applying the linear expectation operator $\mathbb{E}^{Y}[\cdot]$ preserves the inequality. It is clear that monotonicity for arbitrary $T$ is preserved by our time-interpolation scheme. $\square$

Remark 3.2. Our approach is a special case of the following concept: let $X=\left(X_{t}\right)_{t}$ and $Y=\left(Y_{t}\right)_{t}$ be two independent martingales. Then, $Z_{t}:=X_{t} Y_{t}$ is again a martingale and has call prices

$$
\mathbb{E}\left[\left(Z_{t}-K\right)^{+}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\left(X_{t} Y_{t}-K\right)^{+}\right]=\int_{0}^{\infty} \mathbb{E}\left[\left(x Y_{t}-K\right)^{+}\right] \mathbb{P}\left[X_{t}=x\right] d x
$$

mirroring (16). It is worth noting that any classic arbitrage-free pricing model $Y$ can be used for our model. Heston's model [Hes93] for example provides a rich surface with pronounced skew which can be used as a background martingale. $\square$

### 3.1 Production Model

For our proposed production model whose implementation we will discuss in section 4 we propose a very simplistic structure which still fits market data remarkably well: we will basically assume $Y$ is $\log$-normal. Assume that $V_{1}<\cdots<V_{M}$ are ATM variances from the market for model expiries $0<T_{1}<\cdots<T_{M}$ and let $\eta \in[0,1)$ be a parameter which controls the smoothness of our model. Given model strikes $K_{j}^{i}$ our production model is then given as

$$
\begin{equation*}
\hat{C}_{j}(K):=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{j}} q_{j}^{i} \operatorname{Call}\left(K_{j}^{i}, K, \eta V_{j}\right) \tag{17}
\end{equation*}
$$

We then solve for $q$ to globally fit the model within market bid/ask prices for market expiries and strikes.

The parameter $\eta$ allows controlling the desired smoothness of (17): if it is set to zero, the model is equivalent to the linear model discussed in section 2.2. By increasing $\eta$ we can then smoothen our model. In the extreme case $\eta=1$ the model can only fit ATM options. Figures 3, 4 and 5 illustrate three different cases of fits with $\eta=0, \eta=0.25^{2} \approx 0.06$ and $\eta=0.5^{2}=0.25$. The latter is our recommended default setting.

$$

In the log-normal case translates to using different variances per strike:

### 3.2 Efficient Parametrization

Fitting the model (13) given variances $0<V_{1}<\cdots<V_{M}$ means finding a martingale density with marginals $q_{j}$ such that the model prices are within bid/ask spread of observed market data. This can easily be implemented using linear programming as is shown in section 4 which also covers the case of different strikes per expiry (which is the case in practise).

However, if we aim to use our parametrization in generative models then its representation in terms of a martingale density is not convenient as the marginals $q_{1}, \ldots, q_{M}$ have to satisfy the linear constraints of definition 2.9. For such applications we propose parameterizing the martingale density in terms of the discrete local volatilities introduced in [BR15]. This parametrization has been used to great effect in multi-asset arbitrage-free option surface simulators in [WWP+ 21 ], albeit on discrete grids of strikes. With our approach here such generative model is now able to generate smooth arbitrage-free option surfaces.

We start first with the case of homogeneous strikes $0=K^{0}<K^{1}<\cdots<1<\cdots<K^{N}$ across expiries which is much simpler and an ingredient into the inhomogeneous case. This material is from [BR15] with notations aligned.

Theorem 3.4 (Transition Operators from Discrete Local Volatilities). Let $\Sigma_{j}^{i} \geq 0$ for $i= 2, \ldots, N-1$ and $j=1, \ldots, M$ be a surface of input discrete local volatilities. Let

$$
\gamma_{j}^{i+}:=\frac{1}{\frac{1}{2}\left(K^{i+1}-K^{i-1}\right)} \frac{1}{K^{i+1}-K^{i}} \quad \text { and } \quad \gamma_{j}^{i-}:=\frac{1}{\frac{1}{2}\left(K^{i+1}-K^{i-1}\right)} \frac{1}{K^{i}-K^{i-1}}
$$

and define

$$
\begin{equation*}
w_{j}^{i \pm}:=\frac{1}{2}\left(\Sigma_{j}^{i} K^{i}\right)^{2}\left(T_{j}-T_{j-1}\right) \gamma_{j}^{i \pm} \tag{19}
\end{equation*}
$$

and $w_{j}^{1 \pm}=w_{j}^{N \pm}:=0$. Then the inverse $Q_{j \mid j-1}$ of the tri-band matrix
is a martingale transition operator.
Starting in $q_{0}=(1)$ we then obtain the corresponding martingale densities

$$
q_{j}:=Q_{j \mid j-1} \cdot q_{j-1}
$$

The proof is provided in appendix A. 2 on page 27.
Remark 3.5. The tri-band matrix $Q_{j \mid j-1}^{-1}$ can be written in terms of a vector $\Sigma_{j}=\left(0, \Sigma_{j}^{2}, \ldots, \Sigma_{j}^{N-1}, 0\right)$ as follows:

$$
Q_{j \mid j-1}^{-1}=E+\Omega \Sigma_{j}^{2}\left(T_{j}-T_{j-1}\right)
$$

(where the right hand multiplication of the matrix with the vector is column-wise) for pre-computed
matrices
with

$$
\omega_{j}^{i+}:=\frac{\left(K^{i}\right)^{2}}{\left(K^{i+1}-K^{i-1}\right)\left(K^{i+1}-K^{i}\right)} \quad \text { and } \quad \omega_{j}^{i-}:=\frac{\left(K^{i}\right)^{2}}{\left(K^{i+1}-K^{i-1}\right)\left(K^{i}-K^{i-1}\right)} .
$$

$\square$

We now expand our definition to inhomogeneous strikes. Assume therefore we are given general strikes $0=K_{0}<K^{\text {min }}=K_{j}^{1}<\cdots<1<\cdots<K_{j}^{N_{j}}=K^{\text {max }}$ and discrete local volatilities $\Sigma_{j}=\left(\Sigma_{j}^{2}, \ldots, \Sigma_{j}^{N_{j}-1}\right)^{\prime}$ which each imply a transition operator $Q_{j \mid j-1} \in \mathbb{R}^{N_{j} \times N_{j}}$, with the caveat that the latter is defined to transition a density $\bar{q}_{j-1}$ defined over strikes $K_{j}$ into a density $q_{j}$, also defined over $K_{j}$. However, the previous density $q_{j-1}$ is given over strikes $K_{j-1}$. We apply the idea from [BR15] to apply linear interpolation between expiries i.e.

$$
\begin{equation*}
\bar{C}_{j-1}^{\ell}:=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{j-1}} q_{j-1}^{i}\left(K_{j-1}^{i}-K_{j}^{\ell}\right)^{+} \tag{21}
\end{equation*}
$$

for $\ell=0, \ldots, N_{j}$. We note that $\bar{C}_{j-1}^{0}=1$ and $\bar{C}_{j-1}^{N_{j}}=0$. These call prices are arbitrage-free and imply a density

$$
\begin{aligned}
\bar{q}_{j}^{\ell} & :=\frac{\bar{C}_{j-1}^{\ell+1}-\bar{C}_{j-1}^{\ell}}{K_{j}^{\ell+1}-K_{j}^{\ell}}-\frac{\bar{C}_{j-1}^{\ell}-\bar{C}_{j-1}^{\ell-1}}{K_{j}^{\ell}-K_{j}^{\ell-1}} \\
& =\sum_{i=1}^{N_{j-1}} q_{j-1}^{i}\left(\frac{\left(K_{j-1}^{i}-K_{j}^{\ell+1}\right)^{+}-\left(K_{j-1}^{i}-K_{j}^{\ell}\right)^{+}}{K_{j}^{\ell+1}-K_{j}^{\ell}}-\frac{\left(K_{j-1}^{i}-K_{j}^{\ell}\right)^{+}-\left(K_{j-1}^{i}-K_{j}^{\ell-1}\right)^{+}}{K_{j}^{\ell}-K_{j}^{\ell-1}}\right)
\end{aligned}
$$

for $\ell=1, \ldots, N_{j}$ where $K^{N_{j}+1}>K^{N_{j}}$ is arbitrary. The term on the right is $L_{j \mid j-1}^{\ell, i}$ for $\ell=1, \ldots, N_{j}$ and $i=1, \ldots, N_{j-1}$. That means:

Theorem 3.6. Assume that $q_{j-1}$ is a density over $K_{j-1}$ with unit mean. Define the matrix $L_{j \mid j-1} \in \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{N_{j} \times N_{j-1}}$ with elements

$$
\begin{equation*}
L_{j \mid j-1}^{\ell, i}:=\frac{\left(K_{j-1}^{i}-K_{j}^{\ell+1}\right)^{+}-\left(K_{j-1}^{i}-K_{j}^{\ell}\right)^{+}}{K_{j}^{\ell+1}-K_{j}^{\ell}}-\frac{\left(K_{j-1}^{i}-K_{j}^{\ell}\right)^{+}-\left(K_{j-1}^{i}-K_{j}^{\ell-1}\right)^{+}}{K_{j}^{\ell}-K_{j}^{\ell-1}} \geq 0 \tag{22}
\end{equation*}
$$

for $\ell=1, \ldots, N_{j}$ and $i=1, \ldots, N_{j-1}$. Then

$$
\bar{q}_{j-1}=L_{j \mid j-1} q_{j-1} \in \mathbb{R}^{N_{j}}
$$

is a density with unit mean over $K_{j}$ where $\bar{q}_{j-1}$ represents the density implied by linearly interpolated call prices computed using $p_{j-1}$ at strikes $K_{j}$.

Finally,

$$
K_{j} L_{j \mid j-1}=K_{j-1}
$$

The proof that $1 L_{j \mid j-1}=1$ and $K_{j} L_{j \mid j-1}=K_{j-1}$ is in the appendix.

Theorem 3.7 (Implied Discrete Local Volatilities). Assume that the discrete call prices $C_{j}^{i}$ for $j=1, \ldots, M$ and $i=1, \ldots, N_{j}$ are arbitrage-free and define

$$
\begin{equation*}
\Sigma_{j}^{i}:=\sqrt{2 \frac{\Theta_{j}^{i}}{K_{j}^{i 2} \Gamma_{j}^{i}}} \quad \text { with } \quad \Theta_{j}^{i}:=\frac{C_{j}^{i}-\bar{C}_{j-1}^{i}}{T_{j}-T_{j-1}} \quad \text { and } \quad \Gamma_{j}^{i}:=\frac{d C_{j}^{i}-d C_{j}^{i-1}}{\frac{1}{2}\left(K_{j}^{i+1}-K_{j}^{i-1}\right)} \tag{23}
\end{equation*}
$$

for $j=1, \ldots, M$ and $i=2, \ldots, N_{j}-1$ with $0 / 0:=0$. Here, $\bar{C}_{j-1}^{i}$ is again given by linear interpolation of $C_{j-1}$ as in (21).

In this case

$$
\begin{equation*}
w_{j}^{i \pm}=\frac{C_{j}^{i}-\bar{C}_{j-1}^{i}}{p_{j}^{i}} \bar{\gamma}_{j}^{i \pm} \quad \text { with } \quad \bar{\gamma}_{j}^{i+}:=\frac{1}{K_{j}^{i+1}-K_{j}^{i}}, \quad \bar{\gamma}_{j}^{i-}:=\frac{1}{K_{j}^{i}-K_{j}^{i-1}} \tag{24}
\end{equation*}
$$

Then, the transition operator $Q_{j \mid j-1}$ reproduces discrete input call prices $C_{1}^{1}, \ldots, C_{M}^{N}$ in the sense that $p_{j}=Q_{j \mid j-1} \tilde{p}_{j-1}$.

The proof is also provided in appendix A. 2 on page 27.
Essentially the statement of this section is that in order to simulate a smooth arbitrage-free option surface, fix (or also simulate) increasing $0<V_{1}<\cdots<V_{M}$ and a $(N-2) \times M$ grid of discrete non-negative local volatilities $\Sigma_{j}^{i}$. They have the natural scale of annualized volatilities (indeed, as the grid density increases the discrete local volatilities approach Dupire's continuous time local volatility). The only constraint on the discrete local volatilities is non-negativity, making them unique suitable for simulation and descriptive statistics. This is subject to further work.

### 3.3 Smoothed Linear Interpolation within Bid/Ask

In most of this article balance of focus is towards smoothness vs perfection of fit. Indeed, the main purpose of our model is to "fit" a bigger number of market option prices with a smooth model with fewer model strikes and expiries. However, our approach can also be used for what we call "smoothed linear interpolation" within the bid/ask spread following [CRW20a].

For this application we assume the model expiries match market expiries and that our (inner) model strikes $K_{1}^{2}, \ldots, K_{M}^{N_{M}-1}$ correspond to actual market strikes. Assume now that asks $A_{j}^{i}$ and bids $B_{j}^{i}$ for $j=1, \ldots, M$ and $i=2, \ldots, N_{j}-1$ have a non-zero spread. Assume further there exist arbitrage-free prices $C_{j}^{i}$ such that $B_{j}^{i} \leq C_{j}^{i}<A_{j}^{i}$ outside $i=0,1, N_{j}$. (We set $C_{j}^{i}:=\left(1-K_{j}^{i}\right)^{+}$for $i=0,1, N_{j}$ in line with theorem 2.6.) We may find an arbitrage-free $C$ within bid/ask using linear programming. Denote by $p$ the density implied by $C$ via (7).

Similar to our production model, let

$$
\hat{C}_{j}^{i}(\theta):=\sum_{\ell=1}^{N_{j}} p_{j}^{\ell} \operatorname{Call}\left(K_{j}^{\ell}, K_{j}^{i}, \theta T\right)
$$

where $p_{1}, \ldots, p_{M}$ are the martingale densities computed from $C$ via (7).
Proposition 3.8 (Smoothed Linear Interpolation within Bid/Ask). For all $\theta \geq 0$ we have $\hat{C}_{i}^{\ell}(\theta) \geq B_{j}^{i}$ for $i=2, \ldots, N_{j}-1$.

Moreover there exists a maximal $\theta^{*}>0$ such that $\hat{C}$ interpolates within bid/ask in the sense that $B_{j}^{i} \leq \hat{C}_{i}^{\ell}\left(\theta^{*}\right) \leq A_{j}^{i}$ for $i=2, \ldots, N_{j}-1$.

Proof. This is a consequence of the fact that $\hat{C}_{j}^{i}(\theta) \downarrow C_{j}^{i} \geq B_{j}^{i}$ smoothly and monotonic as $\theta \downarrow 0$ for each pair $(j, i)$ with $i=2, \ldots, N_{j}-1$.

### 3.4 Generalization

The model (13) shines due to its simplicity. From a practical point of view it is entirely sufficient for fitting observed market data within bid/ask spreads.

For completeness we present now an extension which provides a more advanced version which features a martingale $Y$ per strike. We focus on a log-normal version for homogeneous strikes $0<K^{1}<\cdots<K^{N}$ for clarity, but other driving models $Y$ can be utilized. We view this model primarily as a conceptual result; in practice the production model already captures observed surfaces to high accuracy.

Theorem 3.9 (Iterative Smooth Call Prices). Let $d V_{j}^{i} \geq 0$ be forward Black-Scholes implied variances for $i=1, \ldots, N$ and $j=1, \ldots, M$. Let $q_{1}, \ldots, q_{M}$ be marginal densities with unit mean (e.g. they do not have to be martingale densities). Define iteratively

$$
\begin{equation*}
\tilde{c}_{1}(K ; v):=\sum_{i_{1}=1}^{N} q_{1}^{i_{1}} \operatorname{Call}\left(K^{i}, K ; d V_{1}^{i}+v\right) \tag{25}
\end{equation*}
$$

and

$$
\begin{equation*}
\tilde{c}_{j}(K ; v):=\sum_{i_{j}=1}^{N} q_{j}^{i_{j}} K^{i_{j}} \tilde{c}_{j-1}\left(\frac{K}{K^{i_{j}}} ; d V_{j}^{i_{j}}+v\right) \tag{26}
\end{equation*}
$$

and then

$$
\begin{equation*}
\tilde{C}_{j}(K):=\tilde{c}_{j+1}(K, 0) \quad \text { and } \quad \tilde{C}(T, K):=\alpha_{j}(T) \tilde{C}_{j+1}(K)+\left(1-\alpha_{j}(T)\right) \tilde{C}_{j}(K) \tag{27}
\end{equation*}
$$

with $\alpha$ such as defined in (8).
Then, $\tilde{C}$ is a smooth arbitrage-free option surface.
The proof is trivial observing that this pricing scheme is realized by

$$
\begin{equation*}
Z_{j}:=\prod_{\ell=1}^{j} X_{\ell} Y_{\ell}^{X_{\ell}} \tag{28}
\end{equation*}
$$

with $X_{\ell} \sim q_{\ell}$ and $Y_{\ell}^{i}:=\exp \left(\sqrt{d V_{\ell}^{i}} N_{\ell}^{i}-\frac{1}{2} d V_{\ell}^{i}\right)$ where $N_{j}^{i}$ is iid standard normal.
Proposition 3.10. We may write

$$
\begin{equation*}
\tilde{C}_{j}(K):=\sum_{i_{j}=1}^{N} q_{j}^{i_{j}}\left(\sum_{i_{j-1}, \ldots, i_{1}} q_{j-1}^{i_{j-1}} \cdots q_{1}^{i_{1}} \operatorname{Call}\left(K^{i_{j}} \cdots K^{i_{1}}, K ; d V_{j}^{i_{j}}+\cdots d V_{1}^{i_{1}}\right)\right) \tag{29}
\end{equation*}
$$

This formula is used in our implementation in section 4.3 on page 22 to iteratively fit the model to market data from the earliest expiry towards the latest expiry (the implementation allows for different strikes per expiry; we omitted this here for sake of notational clarity).

As pleasing as this result is theoretically it is numerically more more expensive to implement than our simple (13) as we need to calculate the full tensor structure on the right hand side of (29) while iterating forward.
Remark 3.11 (Extension to Martingale Densities). Equation (28) shows that our representation means that the jump process $X_{j}$ is independent of $X_{j-1}$. We assumed this for computational efficiency, but it is noteworthy that we can also apply our idea to more general martingales $X$ as follows: assume $q_{1}, \ldots, q_{M}$ is a martingale density with transition operators $Q_{j \mid j-1}$. Define then

$$
\begin{equation*}
\breve{C}_{j}(K):=\sum_{i_{j}=1}^{N} K^{i_{j}}\left(\sum_{i_{j-1}, \ldots, i_{1}} Q_{j \mid j-1}^{i_{j} \mid i_{j-1}} Q_{j-1 \mid j-2}^{i_{j-1} \mid i_{j-2}} \cdots Q_{1 \mid 0}^{i_{1} \mid 0} \operatorname{Call}\left(K^{i_{j}}, K ; d V_{j}^{i_{j}}+\cdots d V_{1}^{i_{1}}\right)\right) \tag{30}
\end{equation*}
$$

Then, $\breve{C}$ has the martingale representation

$$
Z_{j}=X_{j} \prod_{\ell=1}^{j} Y_{\ell}^{X_{\ell}}
$$

It is notable that this approach has a similar expression in a classic diffusion setting:
Remark 3.12 (Generalization to a Diffusion Setting). Assume that

$$
\frac{d Y_{t}^{x}}{Y_{t}^{x}}=u_{t}(x) d W_{t} \quad \text { and } \quad \frac{d X_{t}}{X_{t}}=v\left(X_{t}\right) d B_{t}
$$

are both positive martingales; then

$$
\frac{d Z_{t}}{Z_{t}}=u_{t}\left(X_{t}\right) d W_{t}
$$

is a well-defined martingale. We then have

$$
d\left(X_{t} Z_{t}\right)=X_{t} u_{t}\left(X_{t}\right) Z_{t} d W_{t}+Z_{t} v\left(X_{t}\right) X_{t} d B_{t}=\left(X_{t} Z_{t}\right)\left(u_{t}\left(X_{t}\right) d W_{t}+v\left(X_{t}\right) d B_{t}\right)
$$

which shows that $X Y$ is also a positive martingale (albeit not Markov, and correlated to $X$ ). This idea can be trivially extended to processes with jumps.

## 4 Implementation

We now discuss the practical implementation of fitting our model variants to observed market data. We will start with the simplest variant and work our way to the fully consistent model of theorem 3.9. For both our production and generalized model we provide the ability to specify different strikes per expiry.

### 4.1 Homogeneous Strikes - Toy Model

To illustrate the general mechanics we start by providing a very simple algorithm for the much simpler problem of fitting a model with homogeneous strikes to a market with the same expiries and strikes. This is meant to be explain the basic ideas as it does not suffer from too many indices. In the the following section 4.2 we present a more practical approach which handles inhomogeneous strikes etc. The reader may skip directly to that section.

Assume that we have strikes $0<K^{1}<\cdots<1<\cdots<N$ and expiries $0<T_{1}<\cdots<T_{M}$ with observed market mid-prices $C_{j}^{i}$. We also assume we have specified Black-Scholes implied variances $V_{j}$ which are increasing in expiries, $V_{j} \geq V_{j-1}$ and let $\eta \in[0,1)$ be a variance factor, e.g. $\eta=0.25$. Define the matrix $\mathbf{C}_{j} \in \mathbb{R}^{(N-2) \times N}$ for $j=1, \ldots, M$ as

$$
\begin{equation*}
\mathbf{C}_{j}^{\ell, i}:=\operatorname{Call}\left(K^{i}, K^{\ell}, \eta V_{j}\right) \tag{31}
\end{equation*}
$$

for $\ell=2, \ldots, N-1$ and $i, \ell=1, \ldots, N$. This matrix maps the our model prices given as a linear function of our density $q_{j}$ to market prices. Let also

$$
\mathbf{U}^{\ell, i}:=\left(K^{i}-K^{\ell}\right)^{+}
$$

which are the maps from $q$ to discrete call prices of the $q$ 's themselves. We will use this to ensure that the $q$ 's are increasing in call prices.

The candidate call prices based on $m$ for strikes $K$ at expiry $T_{j}$ are per (17) given in terms of a density $q_{j}$ as

$$
\mathbf{c}_{j}:=\mathbf{C}_{j} \cdot q_{j} \in \mathbb{R}^{N-2}
$$

where as before "." denotes the classic matrix/vector product.

The fitting problem for our model can then be written as the linear program

$$
\begin{cases}\text { Variables: } &  \tag{SMP}\\ q_{1}, \ldots, q_{M} \text { with } q_{j} \in \mathbb{R}^{N} & \text { Marginal densities } \\ & \\ \text { Slack variables: } & \text { Prices at market strikes } \\ \mathbf{c}_{j}:=\mathbf{C}_{j} \cdot q_{j} \in \mathbb{R}^{N-2} & \text { Call prices for } q_{j} \\ \mathbf{u}_{j}:=\mathbf{U}_{j} \cdot q_{j} \in \mathbb{R}^{N} & \\ & \\ \text { Constraints: } & \text { Marginal density with unit mean } \\ q_{j} \geq 0,1^{\prime} \cdot q_{j}=1, K^{\prime} \cdot q_{j}=1 & \text { Martingale condition } \\ \mathbf{u}_{j} \geq \mathbf{u}_{j-1} & \\ \text { Objective: } & \\ \inf q_{1}, \ldots, q_{M}: \sum_{j}\left|w_{j} \cdot\left(C_{j}-\mathbf{c}_{j}\right)\right| & \text { Weighted fit to market }\end{cases}
$$

Our example implementation we provided computes the tensor expressions in every iteration. This means the algorithm has quadratic execution time and is in its current form not suitable for production use.

### 4.2 Production Model for Inhomogeneous Grids

In practice the strikes in the market are not constant in time, in particular not when normalized by the forward into "pure" strikes. When using real market data, it is also sometimes necessary to add additional strikes when market strikes are far away from each other (e.g. after filtering by minimum quoted volumes). The code we used to test our model adds strikes whenever market strikes are further apart than some maximum $d x$, and adds additional strikes outside the observed strike range.

Therefore, assume first that we are given boundary strikes $0<K^{\min }<1<K^{\max }$ which lie outside any observed market prices. Assume further that we are given:

- Market Expiries $0<\tau_{1}<\cdots<\tau_{m}$.
- Market Strikes: For each market expiry we are given $n_{\ell}$ strikes $k_{\ell}^{1}, \ldots, k_{\ell}^{n_{\ell}}$ with $0< K^{\min } \ll k_{\ell}^{1}<\cdots<1<\cdots<k_{\ell}^{n_{j}} \ll K^{\max }$.
- Market Prices: For each $k_{\ell}^{r}$ we are given a target price $C_{\ell}^{r}$, an ask price $A_{\ell}^{r}$ and a bid prices $B_{\ell}^{r} \geq 0$. We assume that the spread is strictly positive: $A_{\ell}^{r}>B_{\ell}^{r}$.
- Weights: For each option we are given a weight $w_{\ell}^{r} \in \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}$, typically the inverse of the prevailing bid/ask spread, or the inverse of Vega to approximate a fit in implied volatilities.
- Market ATM Variances: we assume we are given market ATM log-normal variances $0<v_{1}<\cdots<v_{m}$.

The model is itself defined as before:

- Model Expiries: $0<T_{1}<\cdots<T_{M}$.
- Model Strikes: Each model expiry has $N_{j}$ strikes $0=K^{\text {min }}=K_{j}^{1}<\cdots<1<\cdots< K_{j}^{N_{j}}=K^{\text {max }}$ which may or may not include any market strikes.
- Implied Variances we also assume we have an increasing set of implied variances $0<V_{1}< \cdots<V_{m}$ and associated scaling factor $\eta \in[0,1)$, for example $\eta=0.25$. If $\eta$ is zero, then the model becomes linear. Figure 8 in page 22 illustrates the impact of $\eta$.

As a first step we identify the location of the market expiries with respect to the model expiries. To this end, we interpolate model prices using some weighting function $\alpha$ as in (8). That means
that the model prices for expiry $\tau_{\ell}$ can be written as convex combination of prices at the two surrounding model expiries $T_{j(\ell)-1}<\tau_{\ell} \leq T_{j(\ell)}$ as

$$
C_{\ell}^{r}=\alpha_{\ell} \operatorname{Call}\left(K_{j(\ell)}, k_{\ell}^{r}, \eta v_{\ell}\right)^{\prime} \cdot q_{j(\ell)}+\left(1-\alpha_{\ell}\right) \operatorname{Call}\left(K_{j(\ell)-1}, k_{\ell}^{r}, \eta v_{\ell}\right)^{\prime} \cdot q_{j(\ell)-1}
$$

with weights $\alpha_{\ell}:=\alpha_{j(\ell)}\left(\tau_{\ell}\right)$ if $V_{j(\ell)-1} \leq v_{\ell} \leq V_{j(\ell)}$. We therefore pre-compute matrices $\mathbf{C}_{\ell \mid+} \in \mathbb{R}^{n_{j(\ell)} \times N_{j}}$ and $\mathbf{C}_{\ell \mid-} \in \mathbb{R}^{n_{j(\ell)-1} \times N_{j}}$ as

$$
\begin{align*}
\mathbf{C}_{\ell \mid+}^{r, i} & := & \alpha_{\ell} & \operatorname{Call}\left(K_{j(\ell)}^{i} k_{\ell}^{r}, \eta v_{\ell}\right) \text { and }  \tag{32}\\
\mathbf{C}_{\ell \mid-}^{r, i} & := & \left(1-\alpha_{\ell}\right) & \operatorname{Call}\left(K_{j(\ell)-1}^{i} k_{\ell}^{r}, \eta v_{\ell}\right)
\end{align*}
$$

In order to ensure that call prices are increasing in time we also introduce the matrices $\mathbf{U}_{j} \in \mathbb{R}^{N_{j}, N_{j}}$ and $\mathbf{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{N_{j+1}, N_{j}}$ given as

$$
\begin{align*}
\mathbf{U}_{j}^{\ell, i} & :=\operatorname{Call}\left(K_{j}^{i}, K_{j}^{\ell}, \eta \omega V_{j}\right) \text { and }  \tag{33}\\
\mathbf{R}_{j}^{\ell, i} & :=\operatorname{Call}\left(K_{j-1}^{i}, K_{j}^{\ell}, \eta \omega V_{j-1}\right) .
\end{align*}
$$

The variable $w \in\{0,1\}$ is explained below.
Our linear program then becomes:

$$
\begin{cases}\text { Variables: } & \text { Marginal densities } \\ q_{1}, \ldots, q_{M} \text { with } q_{j} \in \mathbb{R}^{N_{j}} & \\ \text { Slack variables for } \ell=1, \ldots, m: & \\ \mathbf{c}_{\ell}:=\mathbf{C}_{\ell \mid+} \cdot q_{j(\ell)}+\mathbf{C}_{\ell \mid-} \cdot q_{j(\ell)-1} \in \mathbb{R}^{n_{j}} & \text { Prices at market strikes } \\ & \\ \text { Slack variables for } j=1, \ldots, M: & \\ \mathbf{u}_{j}:=\mathbf{U}_{j} \cdot q_{j} \in \mathbb{R}^{N_{j}} & \text { Prices at model strikes } \\ \mathbf{r}_{j}:=\mathbf{R}_{j} \cdot q_{j-1} \in \mathbb{R}^{N_{j}} & \text { Previous model prices at current strikes (MDL) } \\ \text { Constraints: } & \\ q_{j} \geq 0,1^{\prime} \cdot q_{j}=1, K^{\prime} \cdot q_{j}=1 & \text { Marginal density with unit mean } \\ \mathbf{u}_{j} \geq \mathbf{r}_{j} & \text { Martingale condition }(*) \\ \text { Objective: } & \\ \text { inf } q_{1}, \ldots, q_{M}: \sum_{\ell}\left|w_{\ell} \cdot\left(C_{\ell}-\mathbf{c}_{\ell}\right)\right| & \text { Weighted fit to mid }\end{cases}
$$

In order to ensure absence of arbitrage in time condition $(*)$ must be satisfied with $\omega=0$ as this ensures that the discrete strike marginal densities constitute a martingale density, c.f. theorem 2.6 on page 7. However, as noted there this is in fact just a sufficient but not necessary condition for absence of arbitrage. From a practical and numerical perspective it is also more natural to impose the price condition in observed price space, which is the case for $w=1$. However, this does then not guarantee strict absence of arbitrage.
Remark 4.1 (Bid/Ask Spreads). Actual markets do not have a mid-price to fit to; instead we observe bid $B_{j}^{i}$ and ask prices $A_{j}^{i}$ which are a positive spread $A_{j}^{i}-B_{j}^{i}$ apart.

To incorporate bid/ask's, there are a number of trivial approaches to account for bid/ask spreads.

1. Normalize the fitting error by scaling by the observed bid/ask spread i.e.

$$
\begin{equation*}
w_{j}^{i}:=\frac{1}{A_{j}^{i}-B_{j}^{i}} \tag{34}
\end{equation*}
$$

2. enforce the fitted prices to be within bid/ask by adding the constraints $B_{j} \leq C_{j} \leq A_{j}$. This yields the additional linear constraints

$$
\left\{\begin{array}{c}
\text { Additional Constraints: } \\
\max \left\{C_{j}-A_{j}, 0\right\}=0 \\
\max \left\{B_{j}-C_{j}, 0\right\}=0
\end{array}\right.
$$

3. Increase the fitting penalty when the fitted price is outside bid/ask.

$$
\left\{\begin{array}{l}
\text { Alternative Objective: } \\
\inf _{q_{1}, \ldots, q_{M}}: \sum_{j} w_{j}\left(\epsilon\left|C_{j}-\mathbf{c}_{j}\right|+\max \left\{C_{j}-A_{j}, 0\right\}+\max \left\{B_{j}-C_{j}, 0\right\}\right) .
\end{array}\right.
$$

This is our recommended default setting with $\epsilon=10^{-8}$ with using weights (34).
All of these fit into a linear or quadratic programming framework. $\square$

### 4.3 Generalized Model

The final step is to implement fitting for our generalized model as described in theorem 3.9. To lighten notation we assume that model expiries match market expiries; an extension in the spirit of the previous section is straight forward. Equation (28) on page 18 can be written as

$$
\tilde{C}_{j}(K):=\sum_{i_{j}=1}^{N_{j}} q_{j}^{i_{j}}\left(\sum_{i_{j-1}, \ldots, i_{1}=1}^{N_{j-1}, \ldots, N_{1}} q_{j-1}^{i_{j-1}} \cdots q_{1}^{i_{1}} \cdots K_{1}^{i_{1}} \operatorname{Call}\left(K_{j}^{i_{j}} K_{j-1}^{i_{j-1}} ; K_{j}^{\ell} ; d V_{j}^{i_{j}}+\cdots+d V_{1}^{i_{1}}\right)\right) .
$$

That means that in addition to the input assumptions of the previous section we will also assume that for each $j$ there is a vector $d V_{j}^{1}, \ldots, d V_{j}^{N_{j}}$ of model incremental variances each corresponding to one of the $\log$-normal increments $Y_{j}^{i}$ in theorem 3.9.

We will now present an iterative fitting scheme which fits each step using linear programming going forward along expiries.

Define the tensors

$$
\begin{array}{rlll}
\mathbb{V}_{j} & :=d V_{j} \oplus \cdots \oplus d V_{1} & =\left(d V_{j}^{i_{j}}+\cdots+d V_{1}^{i_{1}}\right)_{i_{j}, \ldots, j_{1}} & \\
\mathbb{K}_{j} & :=\mathbb{R}^{N_{j} \times \cdots \times N_{1}} \\
\mathbb{Q}_{j} & :=K_{j} \otimes \cdots \otimes K_{1} & =\left(K_{j}^{i_{j}} \cdots K_{1}^{i_{1}}\right)_{i_{j}, \ldots, j_{1}} & \in \mathbb{R}^{N_{j} \times \cdots \times N_{1}} \\
\mathbb{Q}_{j} \otimes q_{1} & =\left(q_{j}^{i_{j}} \cdots q_{1}^{i_{1}}\right)_{i_{j}, \ldots, j_{1}} & \in \mathbb{R}^{N_{j} \times \cdots \times N_{1}}
\end{array}
$$

and then

$$
\begin{array}{lll}
\mathbf{C}_{j}:=\operatorname{Call}\left(\mathbb{K}_{j}, k_{j}, \eta \mathbb{V}_{j}\right) & \in \mathbb{R}^{n_{j} \times N_{j} \times \cdots \times N_{1}} \\
\mathbf{U}_{j}:=\left(\mathbb{K}_{j}-K_{j}\right)^{+} & \in \mathbb{R}^{N_{j} \times N_{j} \times \cdots \times N_{1}} \\
\mathbf{R}_{j}:=\left(\mathbb{K}_{j-1}-K_{j}\right)^{+} & \in \mathbb{R}^{N_{j} \times N_{j-1} \times \cdots \times N_{1}}
\end{array}
$$

where indexing is as implied by the dimensionality of each tensor.
We note that provided $\mathbb{Q}_{j}$ the inner product

$$
\mathbf{c}_{j}:=\mathbf{C}_{j} \cdot \mathbb{Q}_{j}^{\prime} \in \mathbb{R}^{n_{j}}
$$

gives the model prices for the market strike at the $j$ th expiry. However, $\mathbb{Q}_{j}$ contains products of all the vectors $q_{j}$ such that above is no longer linear or quadratic.

Iterative Linear Programming: we therefore propose the following scheme: assume that we have found $q_{1}, \ldots, q_{j-1}$ and therefore $\mathbb{Q}_{j-1}$, and that we are now looking to fit the marginal density for the $j$ th expiry, $q_{j} \in[0,1]^{N_{j}}$.

$$
\begin{cases}\text { Variables: } & \\ q_{j} \in \mathbb{R}^{N_{j}} & \text { Marginal density } \\ & \\ \text { Slack variables: } & \\ \mathbf{c}_{j}:=\left(\mathbf{C}_{j} \cdot \mathbb{Q}_{j-1}^{\prime}\right) \cdot q_{j} \in \mathbb{R}^{n_{j}} & \text { Prices at market strikes } \\ \mathbf{u}_{j}:=\left(\mathbf{U}_{j} \cdot \mathbb{Q}_{j-1}^{\prime}\right) \cdot q_{j} \in \mathbb{R}^{N_{j}} & \text { Prices at model strikes } \\ \mathbf{r}_{j}:=\left(\mathbf{R}_{j} \cdot \mathbb{Q}_{j-1}^{\prime}\right) \in \mathbb{R}^{N_{j}} & \text { Previous model prices at current strikes } \\ & \\ \text { Constraints: } & \\ q_{j} \geq 0,1^{\prime} \cdot q_{j}=1, K^{\prime} \cdot q_{j}=1 & \text { Marginal density with unit mean } \\ \mathbf{u}_{j} \geq \mathbf{r}_{j} & \text { Martingale condition } \\ \text { Objective: } & \\ \inf q_{j}:\left|w_{j} \cdot\left(C_{j}-\mathbf{c}_{j}\right)\right| & \text { Weighted fit to market }\end{cases}
$$

## 6 Conclusion

We have presented a smooth strictly arbitrage-free parametrization of an option price surface and shown how to efficiently fit such model - and its simpler variant - to market data, illustrating the broad applicability of the method. We have also shown how to incorporate bid/ask spread into our fit. Our models can be written in terms of "volatility" parameters which only have to be positive
in order to make the model smooth and strictly arbitrage-free. We presented an interpretation as a dynamic process to aid intuition behind our approach.

Our approach is a natural extension of linear option surface fitting whenever smoothness is critical to the application.

## A. 1 Absence of Arbitrage

We present a self-contained proof for theorem 2.2 on page 6 which we restate here for convenience:
Theorem A. 1 (Theorem 2.2). A continuous call price surface $C:[0, \infty)^{2} \rightarrow[0, \infty)$ arbitrage-free if and only if

1. The market has unit expectation: $C(T, 0) \equiv \mathbb{E}\left[Z_{T}\right]=1$ for all $T$;
2. zero is unattainable: $\partial_{K} C(T, 0) \equiv-1$ since then $\mathbb{P}\left[Z_{T}=0\right]=0$;
3. call prices ultimately reach zero: $\lim _{K \uparrow \infty} C(T, K)=0$,
4. call prices are convex: $\partial_{K K} C(T, K) \geq 0$; and
5. call prices are increasing in time: $\partial_{T} C(T, K) \geq 0$.

To prove this statement, we first recall per expiry the following proposition 2.3 from [Bue06]::
Proposition A.2. A function $c:[0, \infty) \mapsto[0,1]$ is arbitrage-free in the sense that there exist a random variable $Z$ with unit mean such that $c(K)=\mathbb{E}\left[(Z-K)^{+}\right]$if and only if

1. $c$ is decreasing,
2. $c$ is convex, and
3. $c(0)=1, \lim _{K \uparrow \infty} c(K)=0$ and $c^{\prime}(0):=\lim _{\delta \downarrow 0} \frac{1}{\delta} c(\delta) \geq-1$.

Moreover, if $c^{\prime}(0)=-1$ then $Z$ is positive.
Proof. Since $c$ is convex and decreasing, its right-hand derivative $c^{\prime}$ exists and is right-continuous and non-decreasing with $c^{\prime} \geq-1$. Since $c(0)=1$ and $\lim _{K \uparrow \infty} c(K)=0$ we also conclude that $c$ is non-negative.

Let $f(K):=1+c^{\prime}(K)$, which is a positive, right-continuous and non-decreasing function and which therefore implies the existence of some positive $\sigma$-finite measure via $\tilde{\nu}[(a, b]]:=f(b)-f(a)$ cf. [AB06] theorem 9.47 pg . 354. The properties $\lim _{K \uparrow \infty} c(K)=0$ and $c(K) \geq 0$ imply that $\lim _{K \uparrow \infty} c^{\prime}(K)=0$, hence we obtain that $\nu^{*}:=\tilde{\nu}[(0, \infty)]=\lim _{K \uparrow \infty} f(K)-f(0)=0-c^{\prime}(0) \in[0,1]$. The proof is complete by defining $\nu(A):=\left(1-\nu^{*}\right) \delta_{0}(A)+\tilde{\nu}(A)=\left(1+c^{\prime}(0)\right) \delta_{0}(A)+\tilde{\nu}(A)$ for measurable sets $A \subseteq \mathbb{R}_{0}^{+}$. This measure has unit expectation as a result of the assumption $c(0)=1$.

We note in particular that if and only if $c^{\prime}(0)=-1$ then $\nu$ has mass only in the positive numbers, confirming positivity of $Z$.

## A. 2 Transition Operators with Discrete Local Volatilities

We present a proof for theorem 3.4 on page 15: we start with the following statement which is essentially from [AH11]:

Theorem A. 4 (Construction of Transition Matrices). Assume that $M \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times N}$ is a square matrix with columns adding up to 1 , and all whose off-diagonal elements are non-positive.

Then, its inverse exists, is non-negative, and its columns add up to 1; in other words $M^{-1} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times N}$ is a probability matrix.

Moreover, if $M$ has the martingale property with respect to $K \in \mathbb{R}^{N}$ in the sense that $K^{\prime} M=K^{\prime}$ then $M^{-1}$ has the martingale property with respect to $K$, too, in the sense that $K^{\prime}=K^{\prime} M^{-1}$.

Proof. A matrix $M$ with the above properties is a Z -matrix, i.e. $M^{i, i}>0$ and $M^{i, \ell} \leq 0$ for $i \neq \ell$. Moreover, its columns add up to 1 , which means it is a non-singular M-matrix, c.f. [BP94], chapter 6: equivalent classification $I_{29}$ of non-singular M-Matrices.

Hence, its inverse exists and is non-negative, c.f. [BP94], chapter 6: equivalent classification $N_{38}$ of non-singular M-Matrices.

Finally, $1^{\prime} M=1^{\prime}$ implies $1^{\prime}=1^{\prime} M^{-1}$, i.e. the columns of $M^{-1}$ add up to 1 , too. Similarly, if $K^{\prime} M=K^{\prime}$, then $K^{\prime}=K^{\prime} M^{-1}$.
